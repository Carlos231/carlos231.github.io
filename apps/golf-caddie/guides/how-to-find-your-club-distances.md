## Why not just use an average club distance chart?

Because the chart isn't measuring you. Average distance charts mix golfers with very different swing speeds, clubs, and strikes, so the 7-iron number on a chart may be well off for you. Your own typical distance, measured a few times, is the number that helps you pick a club on the course.

A chart can still be useful for one thing: checking that your clubs are spaced out sensibly. If two clubs land within a couple of yards of each other, one of them may not be earning its place in the bag. That's a gapping question, and your own numbers answer it better too.

## Should you write down carry or total distance?

Carry for irons and wedges, total for driver and woods. Carry is how far the ball flies before it lands, which is what matters over a bunker or onto a green. Total includes the roll, which matters most off the tee. If you have room for both, write both.

| Club type | Number to rely on | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Driver, woods | Total (and carry if you know it) | Off the tee, roll counts. Carry tells you what you can fly over. |
| Hybrids, irons | Carry | Approach shots need to land on the green, and roll varies by green. |
| Wedges | Carry, for a full swing | Write down part-swing distances separately if you use them. |

## How do you measure your club distances on the range?

Hit about 10 balls with each club at a target with a known distance, using your normal swing. Ignore the two longest and two shortest, then note where most of the rest landed. That's your typical carry. A launch monitor makes this faster; flagged targets with yardage signs work too.

A few things keep the numbers honest:

1. **Warm up first.** Your first few swings aren't your normal ones.
2. **Use your normal swing.** Don't swing harder because someone is measuring.
3. **Watch where the ball lands, not where it stops.** Roll on the range won't match the course.
4. **Treat range balls with care.** Range balls aren't always the same as the ball you play, so confirm the numbers on the course.

Here's a worked example with 10 made-up 7-iron carries, in yards:

| Shots | Carries (yards) |
|---|---|
| All 10 | 118, 126, 131, 133, 134, 135, 136, 138, 140, 149 |
| Drop the 2 shortest and 2 longest | 131, 133, 134, 135, 136, 138 |
| Typical carry (average of the middle 6) | 134.5, so write down **135** |

Notice the 149. That was your best one, and it's tempting to write it down. On the course, playing to 149 would leave you short most of the time.

## How do you check your club distances on the course?

Note the yardage before a full shot, from a sprinkler head, a yardage marker, or a GPS or laser device, and which club you hit. After the shot, note roughly where it landed. Over a few rounds, compare those shots with your range numbers and adjust any club that's consistently longer or shorter.

Two tips make this easier:

- **Only count good contact with your normal swing.** A thin or fat shot tells you about that swing, not your club.
- **Write it down after the hole.** Recording club distances during the round is fine under the [Rules of Golf](https://www.randa.org/en/rog/the-rules-of-golf/rule-4), as long as you use them after the round.

Rangefinders and GPS devices are allowed by default under [Rule 4.3a](https://www.randa.org/en/rog/the-rules-of-golf/rule-4), but a competition can ban them with a Local Rule. Measuring elevation changes (a slope function) isn't allowed.

## What should your yardage table look like?

One row per club, with the club name, your typical carry, and total distance for clubs where roll matters. Keep it short enough to read in a few seconds. Add rows for any part-swing wedge shots you use, like a half-swing sand wedge, because those are the distances people forget.

## Can you check your yardages during a round?

Yes. The Rules of Golf (Rule 4.3a) allow "information that was gathered before the round (such as playing information from previous rounds, swing tips or club recommendations)." A yardage card, a note on your phone, or a list in an app all count, as long as the numbers came from before the round.

What you can't do during a round is work out new club advice from this round's shots, like "my 7 iron is flying 10 yards long today, so take an 8." Save those observations for after the round, and update your table then.

## Where should you keep your club distances?

Wherever you'll actually look before a shot. A card in your bag works, and so does a note on your phone.
